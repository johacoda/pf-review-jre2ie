Leprosy and Buruli ulcer

Why this disease is important

Nigeria is one of the few countries that still find many new leprosy cases every year.

Many Nigerian patients already have numb or deformed hands and feet when leprosy is diagnosed.

Buruli ulcer is found in Nigeria too, and about half of the patients in Africa are children.

Consider this case

A 38-year-old farmer comes to the clinic with a burn on his right palm that he didn't feel. For two years he's had many small, shiny, pale patches and a few nodules on his back, arms and face, on both sides. Some patches are numb and others aren't, and his hands and feet have gone numb. His right ring and little fingers have started to curl, and the ulnar nerve above his right elbow is thick and tender.

A slit-skin smear shows many acid-fast bacilli, and he starts rifampicin, dapsone and clofazimine. Three months later he's back with crops of painful red nodules on his arms and thighs, fever and painful elbows. His haemoglobin has fallen since he started treatment.

His 9-year-old daughter swims in the stream behind their farm. She has a painless swelling on her forearm that has broken down into an ulcer with ragged, overhanging edges.

What does his slit-skin smear say about where he sits on the spectrum?

Mycobacterium leprae

Key facts

  • Mycobacterium leprae is an acid-fast rod that has never been grown on artificial culture media.
  • Mycobacterium leprae grows best in cool tissue, so it settles in skin, superficial nerves, nose, eyes and testes.
  • Mycobacterium leprae lives inside macrophages, where antibodies can't reach it.

Mycobacterium leprae causes leprosy, and where the organism can live decides which tissues the disease damages.

Transmission of leprosy

Key facts

  • Leprosy is thought to spread by droplets from the nose and mouth of an untreated patient, after long, close contact.
  • A patient with leprosy stops spreading the organism once treatment starts.
  • Why one exposed person develops leprosy and another doesn't isn't known.

Leprosy passes between people who live closely together for a long time, and only some of those exposed are susceptible.

Connect this to other specialties
Who is at risk of leprosy

Transmission of leprosy, step by step

  1. A patient with untreated leprosy carries Mycobacterium leprae in the nose and mouth, and droplets of those secretions are thought to carry it to other people.

  2. Casual contact doesn't pass leprosy on. The people at risk are those in long, close contact with an untreated patient, such as the people who share the patient's home.

  3. Why one exposed person becomes infected and another doesn't isn't known. The host's cell-mediated immunity then sets how severe the disease becomes.

  4. Once a patient starts treatment they stop spreading Mycobacterium leprae. The risk to a household comes from patients nobody has diagnosed yet.

Watch out. You might expect a disease this feared to spread from a handshake or a shared cup. But leprosy needs long, close contact with an untreated patient.

What's each one's link with catching leprosy?

Quick checkA farmer with leprosy has shared a bed with his wife for ten years, and she has no skin lesions. Why hasn't she caught leprosy?

Exposure isn't enough. Only some people exposed to Mycobacterium leprae become infected, and why isn't known. If she is infected, her cell-mediated immunity sets how severe the disease becomes.

Slit-skin smear and bacterial index

Key facts

  • A slit-skin smear shows whether bacilli are in the skin and how heavy the load is.
  • Dead bacilli still take the Ziehl-Neelsen stain, so the bacterial index counts dead and living bacilli together.
  • A negative slit-skin smear doesn't exclude leprosy, because tuberculoid lesions carry too few bacilli to find.

A slit-skin smear tells you whether there are bacilli in the patient's skin, and how many.

Connect this to other specialties
Bacilli in the skin biopsy

Taking and reading a slit-skin smear in leprosy

  1. Smears are taken from standard sites on the skin, the ear lobe among them.

  2. A modified Ziehl-Neelsen method stains the smear: carbol fuchsin, then acid alcohol, then methylene blue for the background. Mycolic acids in the wall hold the red stain, so the bacilli show up red on blue.

  3. Many fields are read under oil immersion before a smear is called negative, because bacilli can be few and far between.

  4. The report says how heavy the load is, as a grade from 0 to 6+. Dead bacilli still take the stain, so the index counts them along with the living ones.

WHO classification of leprosy

Key facts

  • Any one of the three cardinal signs makes the diagnosis of leprosy.
  • Paucibacillary leprosy means one to five skin lesions, no nerve involved and no bacilli on the smear.
  • More than five lesions, a nerve involved, or bacilli on the smear make a patient multibacillary.

At the clinic leprosy is diagnosed by the cardinal signs, then sorted into two groups by counting the lesions, examining the nerves and reading the smear.

Mycobacterium ulcerans and mycolactone

Key facts

  • Mycobacterium ulcerans destroys tissue with a toxin, mycolactone, while Mycobacterium leprae causes damage through the host's response and bacillary load.
  • Mycolactone, the toxin of Mycobacterium ulcerans, kills cells, holds the immune response back and dulls pain.
  • Mycobacterium ulcerans grows best at about 30 °C, so the disease stays in skin and subcutaneous fat.

Mycobacterium ulcerans damages tissue by secreting a toxin, which Mycobacterium leprae never does.

What the excised tissue shows

Mycobacterium ulcerans is one of the , an acid-fast organism of the environment that secretes and destroys skin and subcutaneous fat.

How mycolactone destroys skin and fat in Buruli ulcer

  1. Mycobacterium ulcerans grows best at about 30 °C, below the body's core temperature, so it stays in the skin and the fat under it instead of spreading through the body.

  2. Mycolactone is a lipid toxin. It does three things to the tissue it reaches: it kills cells, it holds the immune response back, and it dulls pain.

  3. Mycolactone stops cells making and releasing secreted proteins, cytokines among them, so few inflammatory cells are called in.

  4. Fat and dermis die over a wide area with little inflammation and almost no pain, so the lesion grows for weeks before the skin breaks down and the patient comes to hospital.

Mycolactone does three things, and each one shows up in the lesion.

Transmission of Buruli ulcer

Key facts

  • Buruli ulcer occurs near slow-moving or stagnant water: ponds, swamps and dams.
  • Nobody has shown spread of Buruli ulcer from one person to another.
  • About half of the patients with Buruli ulcer in Africa are children.

Buruli ulcer is linked with slow-moving or stagnant water, and it doesn't pass from one person to another.

Risk factors for Buruli ulcer

  • People who live, farm, wade or swim near slow-moving or stagnant water.
  • Children make up about half of the patients with Buruli ulcer in Africa.
  • Lesions sit on exposed parts, the limbs above all.

Watch out. You might expect an infection this destructive to pass from patient to patient. But nobody has shown person-to-person spread of Buruli ulcer, and how the organism gets from water into skin is still unknown.

Quick checkA 9-year-old girl has a Buruli ulcer on her forearm, and her father is being treated for leprosy. Did she catch her ulcer from him?

No. Her ulcer is caused by Mycobacterium ulcerans, picked up from the environment rather than from another person, and her father's disease is caused by a different organism. Contact with slow-moving or stagnant water is the link to ask about.

Key facts

Definition of leprosy and Buruli ulcer

Leprosy and Buruli ulcer are both mycobacterial infections of the skin, but they damage it in different ways.

Leprosy (Hansen disease) is a chronic infection by Mycobacterium leprae of the skin, the peripheral nerves, the upper respiratory mucosa and the eyes. The strength of the host's places each patient on the spectrum.

Buruli ulcer (Mycobacterium ulcerans disease) is a necrotising infection of the skin and subcutaneous fat by Mycobacterium ulcerans. Its toxin, , kills the tissue, suppresses inflammation and dulls pain.

Quick checkBoth organisms are mycobacteria. Where does the tissue damage come from in each disease?

In tuberculoid leprosy, mostly from the host: the granulomas that contain Mycobacterium leprae destroy skin nerves. In lepromatous leprosy, from bacilli multiplying unchecked in cool tissues, and later from immune reactions. In Buruli ulcer, from the toxin: mycolactone kills fat and skin and suppresses the inflammation that would fight it.

Ridley-Jopling classification of leprosy

Key facts

  • Ridley-Jopling runs from tuberculoid (TT) through BT, BB and BL to lepromatous (LL) leprosy.
  • Tuberculoid leprosy has one or two numb patches and no bacilli on the slit-skin smear.
  • Lepromatous leprosy has many lesions on both sides and innumerable bacilli on the slit-skin smear.

The Ridley-Jopling classification puts every patient with leprosy on one spectrum. The patient's cell-mediated immunity sets where they sit, and so what the skin and the smear look like.

Nowhere yet. This is indeterminate leprosy, an early lesion before the patient's immune response has moved toward either pole. Finding an acid-fast bacillus inside a dermal nerve confirms it.

Histology of tuberculoid leprosy

In tuberculoid leprosy, cell-mediated immunity is strong, so macrophages are activated and gather into granulomas. The granulomas seek out and destroy the small nerves of the dermis.

Histology of lepromatous leprosy

Key facts

  • Lepromatous leprosy shows sheets of foamy macrophages (lepra cells) and no organised granulomas.
  • A narrow clear band, the Grenz zone, separates the lepromatous infiltrate from the epidermis.
  • A Fite-Faraco stain shows huge numbers of bacilli in lepromatous leprosy, many packed into globi.

In lepromatous leprosy, macrophages take up the bacilli but aren't activated to kill them. The macrophages fill with bacilli and lipid, and sheets of them fill the dermis.

Step 1 of 5

Mycobacterium leprae is taken up by macrophages in the skin and lives inside them. Antibodies act on microbes outside cells, so they can't reach it there. Only T cells that activate the macrophage can get the bacillus killed.

Antigen presentation

Macrophages present antigens of the bacillus to CD4 helper T cells. How strongly those T cells respond decides where the patient sits on the spectrum, and so how severe the disease is.

Tuberculoid pole

make interferon-γ, which activates macrophages by the classical pathway, so they kill the bacilli they've eaten. Activated macrophages gather into in the skin and nerves. Bacilli are hard to find, and there are only one or two skin lesions.

Lepromatous pole

The patient can't mount a cell-mediated response to Mycobacterium leprae, so macrophages aren't activated to kill. Bacilli multiply inside them, and the macrophages become pale and foamy (). Bacilli spread through the skin and superficial nerves, and to the nose, eyes and testes.

Key facts

Drug resistance and multidrug therapy in leprosy

  1. Dapsone acts against Mycobacterium leprae. But where the bacilli are many, as in lepromatous leprosy, resistant bacilli emerge, especially at low doses. So dapsone isn't given on its own.

  2. Any large population of bacteria holds a few mutants resistant to one drug before treatment starts. A single drug kills the rest and leaves these to grow. Rifampicin alone selects its resistant mutants quickly, especially in active infection.

  3. Rifampicin blocks RNA polymerase, dapsone blocks folate synthesis, and clofazimine is thought to bind mycobacterial DNA. Rifampicin is given with at least one other drug so that resistance doesn't emerge.

  4. Paucibacillary and multibacillary patients take the same three drugs. Multibacillary patients carry far more bacilli, and they take the drugs for 12 months instead of 6.

Watch out. A patient with a few pale patches can look too mildly ill for three drugs. But paucibacillary leprosy is treated with the same three drugs as multibacillary leprosy.

Microbiology and Parasitology

Tell me what you know about Mycobacterium leprae.

It's an acid-fast rod that causes leprosy. It has never been grown on artificial media, only in mouse footpads, armadillos and monkeys. It lives inside macrophages and grows best in cool tissue, so it picks the skin, the superficial nerves, the nose, the eyes and the testes. How severe the disease becomes is set by the host's cell-mediated immunity.

  1. What does acid-fast mean, and what makes the organism acid-fast?

    The bacillus keeps the red carbol fuchsin when acid alcohol is poured over the smear. Mycolic acids in the wall don't dissolve in acid alcohol, so the rods stay red while the background takes the methylene blue.

  2. A patient has numb patches and a thickened nerve at the elbow. Why those sites?

    Mycobacterium leprae grows best below the body's core temperature, so it settles where the tissue is coolest: the skin, and the nerves running just beneath it, such as the ulnar nerve above the elbow.

  3. How is leprosy passed from one person to another?

    It's thought to spread by droplets from the nose and mouth of an untreated patient, after long and close contact. Casual contact doesn't pass it on, and a patient stops spreading the organism once treatment starts.

  4. What does a slit-skin smear add once you've examined the patient?

    It shows whether bacilli are in the skin and how many, as a bacterial index from 0 to 6+. Bacilli on the smear make the patient multibacillary whatever the lesion count, and a negative smear doesn't exclude leprosy.

Tell me how you'd confirm a Buruli ulcer.

The specimen comes from the lesion: a swab from the ulcer, or tissue if the lesion is excised. A Ziehl-Neelsen smear can be read the same day but misses many true cases, so IS2404 PCR, run at a reference laboratory, is the standard test for confirming it. Culture is slow, and histopathology needs an operation.

  1. What would the histology show?

    Dead subcutaneous fat and dermal collagen over a wide area, clumps of acid-fast bacilli in the dead tissue, and little inflammation for so much necrosis.

  2. Why is the lesion painless?

    Mycolactone dulls pain directly, by acting on nerve cells. A leprosy patch is numb because the nerve is damaged, but a Buruli ulcer is painless without that, so patients come late.

  3. Where would this child have picked up the organism?

    From the environment near slow-moving or stagnant water: a pond, a swamp or a dammed stream. Farming, wading and swimming are the activities linked with it, and there's no spread from person to person.

Morbid Anatomy

Tell me what you know about the spectrum of leprosy.

Leprosy runs on one spectrum set by the host's cell-mediated immunity. At the tuberculoid pole, strong immunity gives one or two numb, hairless patches and no bacilli on the slit-skin smear. At the lepromatous pole there's no cell-mediated response, so there are many lesions on both sides and innumerable bacilli. The borderline groups lie between and are unstable.

  1. Name the five Ridley-Jopling groups.

    Tuberculoid (TT), borderline tuberculoid (BT), mid-borderline (BB), borderline lepromatous (BL) and lepromatous (LL).

  2. Which patient has the numbest skin lesions, and why might that surprise you?

    The tuberculoid patient: sensation in the patch is completely lost. The lepromatous patient carries far more bacilli, but sensation in the skin lesions is kept, and the numbness is in the hands and feet.

Tell me how you'd tell tuberculoid from lepromatous leprosy on a skin biopsy.

Tuberculoid leprosy shows well-formed noncaseating epithelioid granulomas ringed by lymphocytes, sometimes with Langhans giant cells. They reach the epidermis and destroy dermal nerves, and bacilli are few or absent. Lepromatous leprosy shows sheets of foamy macrophages, no organised granulomas, a Grenz zone under the epidermis, scant lymphocytes and innumerable bacilli, many in globi.

  1. Which stain would you ask for, and why that one?

    A Fite-Faraco stain. Mycobacterium leprae is only weakly acid-fast, and a standard Ziehl-Neelsen stain on a tissue section can miss it.

  2. Where on the lesion would you take the biopsy?

    From the active, raised edge of the newest lesion, deep enough to include deep dermis and subcutis.

Why does a patient with leprosy lose his fingers?

The bacillus doesn't eat them. Mycobacterium leprae damages the cool peripheral nerves near the skin, so the hands and feet lose sensation. Injuries and burns go unfelt, ulcers and infection follow, and repeated damage scars and resorbs the digits.

  1. Which nerves would you feel for, and where?

    The greater auricular, the ulnar above the elbow, the common peroneal at the fibular head and the posterior tibial.

  2. Which nerve is damaged in foot drop, and where would you feel it?

    The common peroneal nerve, thickened where it winds round the fibular head.

Tell me about the pathology of Buruli ulcer.

Buruli ulcer is a necrotising infection of skin and subcutaneous fat by Mycobacterium ulcerans. It starts as a nodule, plaque or oedema and becomes an ulcer with undermined edges. Histology shows wide necrosis of fat and dermal collagen, few inflammatory cells, and clumps of acid-fast bacilli outside cells in the necrotic fat. The toxin mycolactone causes the necrosis and suppresses the inflammation.

  1. Why is a Buruli ulcer painless?

    Mycolactone dulls pain in the lesion. A damaged nerve isn't the cause, unlike in leprosy.

  2. Where do the bacilli lie, compared with lepromatous leprosy?

    Outside cells in the necrotic fat. In lepromatous leprosy they're inside macrophages.

Haematology and Immunology

Tell me what you know about the immune spectrum of leprosy.

The form of leprosy depends on the patient's cell-mediated response to Mycobacterium leprae. At the tuberculoid pole Th1 cells make interferon-γ, macrophages are activated to kill, granulomas form and bacilli are few. At the lepromatous pole there's no effective cell-mediated response, so macrophages fill with bacilli. The borderline groups lie between.

  1. Why don't antibodies protect the lepromatous patient?

    Antibodies act on microbes outside cells. Mycobacterium leprae lives inside macrophages, where only T-cell activation of the macrophage gets it killed.

Tell me about the lepromin test.

Mycobacterium leprae antigens are injected into the skin, and the delayed hypersensitivity response is read, as with the tuberculin test. It's strongly positive in tuberculoid leprosy and negative in borderline and lepromatous leprosy. It places a patient on the spectrum but doesn't diagnose leprosy.

  1. Why doesn't a negative test rule out leprosy?

    Lepromatous patients don't react, so a negative test fits a patient full of bacilli as well as someone without leprosy.

Tell me what you know about the type 1 leprosy reaction.

A type 1 or reversal reaction is an intensified cell-mediated response to Mycobacterium leprae, a delayed (type IV) hypersensitivity reaction. Type 1 reactions occur mainly in borderline leprosy (BT, BB, BL), most often early in multidrug therapy. Existing patches swell and redden, and neuritis can cause sudden nerve loss.

  1. Why does a patient on treatment suddenly lose the use of a foot?

    A type 1 reaction inflames the nerve, here the common peroneal nerve, and motor function can be lost within days: foot drop.

  2. What does a biopsy show?

    Oedema of the granulomas and dermis, with plasma cells and giant cells.

Tell me what you know about erythema nodosum leprosum.

Erythema nodosum leprosum is the type 2 leprosy reaction, in borderline lepromatous and lepromatous leprosy. Immune complexes deposit in tissue and activate complement, a type III reaction. Crops of painful red nodules appear with fever, and neuritis, orchitis, arthritis, iritis and lymphadenopathy can follow.

  1. What does a biopsy of a nodule show?

    Neutrophils with vasculitis or panniculitis, among foamy macrophages holding fragmented bacilli.

  2. How does it differ from a type 1 reaction?

    A type 1 reaction is type IV, mainly in borderline patients, and swells existing patches. Erythema nodosum leprosum is type III, in BL and LL, and brings new nodules with whole-body illness.

A man on treatment for leprosy becomes anaemic. Take me through it.

Dapsone is an oxidant. G6PD makes the NADPH that keeps glutathione reduced, and without enough of it haemoglobin is oxidised into Heinz bodies. The spleen removes them, leaving bite and blister cells, and the cells break down inside the vessels with dark urine. A G6PD assay during the crisis can read falsely normal.

  1. Why can the G6PD assay read normal during the crisis?

    The old deficient cells have been destroyed, and the young cells replacing them hold more enzyme. A repeat after the crisis shows the low level.

  2. Can dapsone cause haemolysis with normal G6PD?

    Yes. High doses cause oxidative haemolysis with Heinz bodies even when G6PD is normal, and many patients on dapsone have some haemolysis.

Tell me what you know about the paradoxical reaction in Buruli ulcer.

A paradoxical reaction is a Buruli ulcer that enlarges during effective treatment. As the bacilli die, mycolactone falls and immune cells return to the lesion, and the inflammation they bring enlarges it. It's a sign that treatment is working, not drug failure.

  1. What held the inflammation back before treatment?

    Mycolactone, the toxin of Mycobacterium ulcerans, which suppresses the immune response in the lesion.

Pharmacology

Tell me what you know about dapsone.

A sulfone used in multidrug therapy for leprosy. Like the sulfonamides, it's a PABA analogue that blocks dihydropteroate synthase, so the bacterium can't make folate. Many patients develop some haemolysis, worst in G6PD deficiency, and methaemoglobinaemia is common.

  1. Why is dapsone never given alone for leprosy?

    Resistance emerges in large populations of Mycobacterium leprae, as in lepromatous leprosy, especially at low doses. So it's always given with rifampicin and clofazimine.

  2. Why doesn't dapsone stop human cells making folate?

    Human cells don't make folate. They take in ready-made folate from the diet, but the bacterium must make its own from PABA.

  3. How would you recognise dapsone methaemoglobinaemia?

    The patient looks cyanosed. Dapsone oxidises the iron in haemoglobin from Fe2+ to Fe3+. It's common but usually not serious.

Tell me what you know about multidrug therapy for leprosy.

Rifampicin, dapsone and clofazimine taken together, by every patient. Paucibacillary leprosy is treated for 6 months and multibacillary leprosy for 12. The drugs are combined because dapsone alone selected resistant bacilli.

  1. How does rifampicin act?

    It binds the beta subunit of bacterial DNA-dependent RNA polymerase and stops RNA synthesis. Human RNA polymerase doesn't bind it, and it kills mycobacteria.

  2. Which drug interaction would you warn a young woman about?

    Rifampicin strongly induces liver cytochrome P450 enzymes, so oral contraceptives are cleared faster and may fail.

  3. What does clofazimine do to the skin?

    It's a red dye that deposits in the skin and conjunctivae and colours them red. Leprosy lesions turn tan to black.

Tell me about the drugs used in erythema nodosum leprosum.

Multidrug therapy carries on. Thalidomide inhibits TNF-α and calms the reaction; prednisolone suppresses inflammatory cytokines and leucocyte entry into tissue; clofazimine adds an anti-inflammatory action.

  1. What's the most serious harm of thalidomide?

    Teratogenesis. Exposure in early pregnancy, while the limbs are forming, causes phocomelia, shortened or absent limbs. It also causes peripheral neuropathy.

  2. Which harms of prednisolone would you watch for over a long course?

    Hyperglycaemia, osteoporosis, muscle wasting, peptic ulcer and masked infection. It can also reactivate dormant tuberculosis.

    2,3-diphosphoglycerate (2,3-DPG)
    A molecule in red cells that lowers haemoglobin's hold on oxygen. A rise shifts the oxygen dissociation curve to the right, so more oxygen is released to the tissues.
    Acholuric jaundice
    Jaundice with no bilirubin in the urine, because the extra bilirubin is unconjugated and bound to albumin. Haemolysis is a cause.
    Acid-fast
    Holding the red carbol fuchsin stain after acid alcohol is poured over the smear. Mycolic acids in the mycobacterial wall don't dissolve in acid alcohol, so the bacilli stay red while everything else turns blue.
    Acute-phase protein
    A plasma protein, such as CRP or fibrinogen, that the liver makes in larger amounts when cytokines signal inflammation.
    Adjusted calcium (albumin-adjusted calcium)
    The total plasma calcium corrected for the albumin level. Much of the calcium is bound to albumin, so a low albumin lowers the total calcium while the free, active calcium stays normal.
    Advanced glycation end products (AGEs)
    Products formed when glucose-derived molecules bind proteins without an enzyme. In diabetes mellitus they cross-link matrix proteins and act on their receptor, RAGE, to damage vessel walls.
    Adynamic bone disease
    Bone with very little formation or resorption, so it can't buffer calcium. In chronic kidney disease it can follow PTH suppressed too far, and the plasma calcium runs high.
    Agglutination
    Visible clumping of particles, such as bacteria or coated beads, when antibody in a serum binds the antigen on their surface. The Widal test is read as agglutination.
    Albumin to creatinine ratio (ACR)
    Urine albumin divided by urine creatinine on a spot sample, in mg/mmol. Dividing by creatinine corrects for how concentrated the urine is, so no timed collection is needed.
    Aleukaemic leukaemia
    Acute leukaemia with no blasts in the blood. The marrow is full of blasts, so a marrow sample makes the diagnosis.
    Alkaline phosphatase (ALP)
    An enzyme found mainly in osteoblasts and the cells lining the bile ducts, and also in gut, kidney and placenta. In adults, plasma ALP comes mostly from bone and liver.
    Red cell alloimmunisation
    The formation of antibodies against antigens on transfused red cells that the patient's own red cells lack.
    Anaemia of chronic disease
    A mild, normocytic anaemia of long-lasting infection, inflammation or cancer. Hepcidin holds iron in macrophages, so the marrow is short of iron while stores are full.
    Angioedema
    Sudden swelling of the deeper layers of the skin and mucosa, often of the lips, tongue or throat. ACE inhibitors cause it through bradykinin.
    Anion gap
    Sodium plus potassium, minus chloride and bicarbonate, in the plasma. It stands for the anions that aren't measured, and it widens when an acid such as lactate replaces bicarbonate.
    Anitschkow cell (caterpillar cell)
    A plump, activated macrophage in an Aschoff body, with abundant cytoplasm and chromatin condensed into a slender, wavy ribbon down the middle of the nucleus.
    Titre
    The furthest a serum can be diluted and still show a reaction. A titre of 1/160 means the reaction was still visible at one part serum in 160.
    Aplastic anaemia
    Pancytopenia from a hypoplastic bone marrow, in which fat replaces the blood-forming tissue.
    Apoptosis
    Regulated cell death: the cell switches on enzymes that dismantle it from within, in pieces that are eaten without inflammation.
    Aristolochic acid
    A compound in Aristolochia plants, used in some herbal medicines. It damages the kidney and causes cancer.
    Aromatase inhibitor
    A drug that blocks aromatase, the enzyme that converts androgens to oestrogens, so less oestrogen is made. Anastrozole and letrozole are non-steroidal; exemestane is a steroid that blocks the enzyme irreversibly.
    Aschoff body
    A focus of inflammation pathognomonic of acute rheumatic fever: T lymphocytes, scattered plasma cells and Anitschkow cells around a zone of fibrinoid necrosis.
    ASO titre (anti-streptolysin O titre)
    The level of antibody against streptolysin O in serum. A raised titre shows a recent group A streptococcal infection.
    Atherosclerosis
    Disease of large and medium-sized arteries in which raised plaques, a lipid core under a fibrous cap, form in the intima and narrow the lumen.
    Atrophy
    A decrease in the size of an organ or tissue, from smaller cells, fewer cells, or both.
    Auer rod
    A needle-shaped pink-red rod in the cytoplasm of a myeloid blast, formed from its granules. Auer rods are pathognomonic of acute myeloid leukaemia.
    Autosplenectomy
    Loss of the spleen to repeated infarcts, which shrink it to a small nubbin of fibrous tissue. In sickle cell disease it's complete by adulthood.
    Avascular necrosis (osteonecrosis)
    Infarction of bone and marrow. Under a joint, a wedge of dead bone forms and can collapse, as in the femoral head.
    Azotaemia
    A raised plasma urea and creatinine from a fall in glomerular filtration rate. When azotaemia is severe enough to cause symptoms and signs, it's called uraemia.
    Bacterial index
    How heavily a slit-skin smear is loaded with bacilli, graded 0 to 6+ on a logarithmic scale. Each step up the scale is about ten times as many bacilli, and dead bacilli are counted as well as living ones.
    Beta-haemolysis (β-haemolysis)
    Complete lysis of the red cells in blood agar around a bacterial colony, leaving a clear zone. Alpha-haemolysis is partial lysis, with a green zone.
    Bite cell
    A red cell with a gap at its edge, left where the spleen has pulled out a Heinz body.
    Blackwater fever
    Sudden intravascular haemolysis in falciparum malaria, with haemoglobin in the urine turning it dark, and acute kidney injury.
    Blast
    An immature blood cell at the earliest stage that can be recognised, before it matures into a working cell. Acute leukaemia is a clone of blasts that can't mature.
    Blast crisis
    The end stage of chronic myeloid leukaemia, when blasts make up 20% or more of the blood or marrow. It behaves like an acute leukaemia, myeloid in most patients and lymphoid in a minority.
    Blister cell
    A red cell whose haemoglobin has pulled away from part of the membrane, leaving a clear blister at one edge. It's seen in G6PD deficiency after oxidant stress.
    Blood schizonticide
    An antimalarial drug that kills the asexual parasites inside red cells, the stage that causes the illness. Every drug that treats malaria is one.
    Bradykinin
    A peptide that dilates blood vessels. Angiotensin-converting enzyme breaks it down, so ACE inhibitors let it build up, which causes their dry cough and angioedema.
    Burr cell (echinocyte)
    A red cell with short, evenly spaced projections round its edge. Burr cells appear in severe uraemia, in liver disease, after splenectomy and as a storage artefact.
    C-peptide
    A peptide the pancreas releases along with insulin. It's raised when the patient's own pancreas makes too much insulin, and suppressed when insulin is injected.
    Calcimimetic
    A drug that activates the calcium-sensing receptor of the parathyroid gland as calcium does, so the gland secretes less PTH. Cinacalcet is one.
    Calcium-sensing receptor
    A receptor, found most densely on the parathyroid glands, that senses the plasma calcium. When it's activated, the gland secretes less PTH. Cinacalcet activates it.
    Carcinoma in situ
    Malignant epithelial cells that haven't crossed the basement membrane. They can't invade the stroma or vessels, so they can't metastasise.
    Caseous necrosis (caseation)
    Necrosis in which dead tissue loses all structure and becomes soft, granular and cheese-like. Down the microscope it has no nuclei and no preserved architecture.
    Caspase
    One of a family of enzymes that carry out apoptosis.
    CD21
    A receptor on B cells for the complement fragment C3d. Epstein-Barr virus binds CD21 to get into the B cell.
    CD40
    A receptor on B cells. A helper T cell engages it through CD40 ligand to make the B cell divide. LMP1 of Epstein-Barr virus mimics CD40 and is switched on all the time.
    Cell cycle–nonspecific drug
    A cytotoxic drug that kills cells whether they're cycling or resting in G0, such as cyclophosphamide or doxorubicin. Cycling cells are still more sensitive to it.
    Cell cycle–specific drug
    A cytotoxic drug that kills cells only while they pass through one phase of the cell cycle: methotrexate and cytarabine in S phase, vincristine in M phase.
    Cell-mediated immunity
    Defence carried out by T cells. Sensitised T cells activate macrophages so that they can kill the organisms they have phagocytosed.
    Chronic carrier
    Someone who keeps harbouring Salmonella Typhi after recovery, usually in the gallbladder, and sheds it in stool while feeling perfectly well.
    Cinchonism
    Tinnitus, headache, nausea, dizziness, flushing and blurred vision caused by quinine at treatment doses. Quinine comes from the bark of the cinchona tree.
    CKD mineral and bone disorder (CKD-MBD)
    The calcium, phosphate, vitamin D and PTH disturbance of chronic kidney disease, with the bone disease and vessel calcification it causes.
    Class switching
    A change in the heavy-chain constant region a B cell uses, for example from IgM to IgG. It cuts and rejoins the heavy-chain gene in the germinal centre.
    Claw hand
    Fingers held curled because the small muscles of the hand have lost their nerve supply. In leprosy it follows damage to the ulnar nerve.
    Clone
    A group of cells that all descend from one cell and carry its mutations. Every cell of a leukaemia belongs to one clone.
    Coagulative necrosis
    Necrosis in which the dead cells keep their outline for some days, so the tissue's architecture can still be seen.
    Cockcroft-Gault equation
    An equation that estimates creatinine clearance from one plasma creatinine, with age, weight and sex. It's used to adjust the dose of drugs the kidney clears.
    Cofactor
    A cause that helps a tumour arise but can't produce it alone. Epstein-Barr virus is a cofactor in endemic Burkitt lymphoma.
    Comedonecrosis
    Necrosis in the centre of a duct filled with high-grade carcinoma in situ. The necrotic debris often calcifies.
    Commissural fusion
    Scarring that joins neighbouring valve leaflets or cusps at their edges, so the valve can't open fully. It's a sign of past inflammation.
    Counter-regulatory hormones
    Hormones that raise plasma glucose and oppose insulin: glucagon, adrenaline, cortisol and growth hormone.
    Creatinine clearance
    The volume of plasma cleared of creatinine each minute, worked out from a timed urine collection and a plasma creatinine. It reads a little above the true GFR, because the tubules secrete some creatinine.
    Culture-negative endocarditis
    Infective endocarditis in which no organism grows from blood cultures, usually because antibiotics were given first or the organism is hard to grow.
    Cyclooxygenase (COX)
    The enzyme that turns arachidonic acid into prostaglandins and, in platelets, thromboxane. Aspirin and other NSAIDs block it, which lowers inflammation, pain and fever.
    Cystatin C
    A small protein made at a steady rate and filtered by the glomeruli, but not secreted by the tubules. Its plasma level is thought not to depend on age or sex, so it can mark GFR.
    Cytochrome P450
    A family of liver enzymes that break down many drugs, warfarin among them. A drug that inhibits these enzymes raises the level of other drugs; a drug that induces them lowers it.
    Cytostatic drug
    A drug that stops cancer cells growing without killing them, so it has to be taken for a long time. Tamoxifen and the aromatase inhibitors are cytostatic.
    D-dimer
    A fragment released when cross-linked fibrin is broken down. A high D-dimer shows that clots are forming and being dissolved, as in DIC.
    Dactylitis (hand-foot syndrome)
    Painful swelling of the hands and feet caused by infarcts of their small bones. It's often the first presentation of sickle cell disease.
    Desmoplasia
    A dense fibrous reaction that an invading carcinoma provokes in the stroma around it. It makes the tumour hard.
    Differentiation syndrome
    A complication of ATRA or arsenic trioxide in acute promyelocytic leukaemia: as the promyelocytes mature, the surge of neutrophils causes fever, hypoxia with lung infiltrates and fluid overload with weight gain.
    Dihydrofolate reductase
    The enzyme that reduces dihydrofolate back to tetrahydrofolate, the active form of folate used to make DNA. Proguanil blocks the malaria parasite's own form of it.
    Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC)
    Clotting set off throughout the circulation, which uses up platelets, fibrinogen and clotting factors faster than they're made, so the patient bleeds.
    DNA gyrase (topoisomerase II)
    A bacterial enzyme that relaxes supercoiled DNA so it can be copied and transcribed. Fluoroquinolones such as ciprofloxacin block it.
    Duffy antigen (DARC)
    A chemokine receptor on the red cell surface. Plasmodium vivax uses it to enter the red cell, and most people of West African origin lack it.
    Dystrophic calcification
    Calcium deposited in dead or damaged tissue while the blood calcium is normal.
    E-cadherin
    A membrane protein that holds epithelial cells together. Lobular carcinoma cells lose its function, so they don't stick to each other.
    EBER (EBV-encoded small RNA)
    Small RNA molecules made in large amounts by Epstein-Barr virus in a latently infected cell. A stain for EBER on a tissue section shows which cells carry the virus.
    Efferent arteriole
    The arteriole that carries blood out of the glomerulus. Angiotensin II constricts it, which keeps the pressure in the glomerular capillaries up.
    Estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR)
    A glomerular filtration rate calculated from the plasma creatinine, age and sex with an equation, in mL/min/1.73 m², instead of measured.
    Epithelial sodium channel (ENaC)
    The sodium channel in the luminal membrane of the collecting tubule. Potassium secretion there is coupled to sodium entry through it, so amiloride, which blocks it, keeps potassium in.
    Encapsulated bacteria
    Bacteria wrapped in a polysaccharide capsule that resists phagocytosis, such as Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae type b and Neisseria meningitidis. Antibody against the capsule lets phagocytes take them up.
    End-stage kidney disease (end-stage renal disease, ESRD)
    Chronic kidney disease so advanced that renal function is lost for good, and only dialysis or a transplant can keep the patient alive.
    Endoperoxide bridge
    The bridge of two linked oxygen atoms in the artemisinin molecule. Iron is thought to split it, and the free radicals released kill the malaria parasite.
    Enteric fever
    The systemic illness caused by Salmonella Typhi and Salmonella Paratyphi, in which the organism leaves the gut, multiplies in lymphoid tissue and macrophages, and reaches the blood. Typhoid fever is enteric fever caused by S. Typhi.
    Epithelioid macrophage
    An activated macrophage with abundant cytoplasm and an elongated nucleus. These cells form the wall of the granuloma.
    Erythema marginatum
    A red, ring-shaped rash on the skin, one of the major Jones criteria for acute rheumatic fever.
    Erythema nodosum leprosum (ENL)
    The type 2 leprosy reaction, in borderline lepromatous and lepromatous leprosy. Immune complexes deposit in tissue and activate complement, causing crops of painful red nodules, fever and inflammation of nerves, joints, eyes and testes.
    Erythropoietin (EPO)
    A glycoprotein hormone, made mostly by peritubular interstitial cells of the renal cortex, that makes the marrow produce more red cells. Its output rises when oxygen delivery to the kidney falls.
    Euglycaemic diabetic ketoacidosis
    Diabetic ketoacidosis with a plasma glucose that's normal or only a little raised. SGLT2 inhibitors can cause it in patients who are very short of insulin.
    Extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL)
    A bacterial enzyme that breaks down beta-lactam drugs, third-generation cephalosporins such as ceftriaxone among them. Strains that make one aren't susceptible to ceftriaxone.
    Extensively drug-resistant (XDR) typhoid
    Salmonella Typhi that resists chloramphenicol, ampicillin and co-trimoxazole, and the fluoroquinolones and third-generation cephalosporins as well.
    Extramedullary haematopoiesis
    Blood cells made outside the bone marrow, usually in the spleen or liver. In chronic myeloid leukaemia it turns the red pulp of the spleen into tissue that looks like marrow.
    Extranodal
    Arising outside the lymph nodes, in an organ such as the jaw, bowel or kidney. Burkitt lymphoma usually arises at extranodal sites.
    Extravascular haemolysis
    Destruction of red cells by macrophages, rather than inside the blood vessels. It causes jaundice and pigment gallstones.
    Facultative intracellular organism
    An organism that can survive and multiply inside a host cell, usually a macrophage, as well as outside it.
    Fat necrosis
    Death of fat cells, from released lipases or from injury, with chalky white deposits where fatty acids bind calcium.
    Fc receptor
    A receptor on macrophages, neutrophils and other leucocytes that binds the tail (Fc part) of an antibody, so the cell can engulf or attack what the antibody has coated.
    Ferritin
    The protein that stores iron. Its plasma level usually follows the iron stores, but it's an acute-phase protein, so inflammation raises it too.
    Fetal warfarin syndrome
    Birth defects, chiefly abnormal bone formation, after a fetus is exposed to warfarin early in pregnancy. Warfarin crosses the placenta and blocks the carboxylation of fetal bone proteins.
    Fibroblast growth factor 23 (FGF23)
    A hormone from bone that makes the kidney excrete phosphate and cuts its production of active vitamin D.
    Fibrinoid necrosis
    Necrosis of a vessel wall where immune complexes or leaked plasma proteins are deposited, staining bright pink.
    Fluorescence in situ hybridisation (FISH)
    A test that binds fluorescent DNA probes to chosen genes in a cell's nucleus. A probe pair can show a gene broken apart or moved to a new chromosome, even in cells that aren't dividing.
    Fite-Faraco stain
    A modified acid-fast stain for tissue sections. It shows the weakly acid-fast Mycobacterium leprae, which a standard Ziehl-Neelsen stain can miss.
    Flow cytometry
    A test that reads fluorescent antibodies bound to cells in suspension, cell by cell. It works on blood, marrow aspirate or disaggregated lymph node tissue.
    Focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS)
    Sclerosis of some glomeruli (focal) that involves only part of each affected tuft (segmental). It's a pattern of injury with many causes, HIV among them.
    Free radical
    A molecule with an unpaired electron, which reacts with and damages lipids, proteins and DNA.
    Fructosamine
    Glucose bound to plasma proteins, mostly albumin. It reflects glucose control over the last few weeks, and it's used when HbA1c can't be trusted.
    Functional asplenia (hyposplenism)
    A spleen that's present but can't do its work of filtering the blood and clearing bacteria. In sickle cell disease it starts in early childhood, while the spleen is still large.
    Functional iron deficiency
    Too little iron reaching the marrow while the iron stores are full. Hepcidin holds the iron in macrophages, as in chronic inflammation and chronic kidney disease.
    Granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF)
    A growth factor that makes committed neutrophil precursors divide and mature. Given as a drug after chemotherapy, it shortens neutropenia.
    Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD)
    The red cell enzyme that makes NADPH. NADPH keeps glutathione reduced, and reduced glutathione protects haemoglobin and the membrane from oxidants.
    Gametocide
    An antimalarial drug that kills the sexual forms of the parasite, the gametocytes, so they can't pass the infection on to a mosquito.
    Gametocyte
    The male or female sexual form of Plasmodium in red cells. A biting mosquito takes gametocytes up, and the cycle goes on in the mosquito.
    Gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT)
    An enzyme of the liver and bile duct cells, not bone. A raised GGT beside a raised ALP means the ALP is coming from the liver. Alcohol and some drugs, such as phenytoin, induce GGT, so a raised GGT alone doesn't prove liver disease.
    Gangrene
    A clinical term for necrosis of a limb or part, usually from loss of blood supply.
    Gene amplification
    Many extra copies of one gene in a cell, so the cell makes far more of the protein it codes for.
    Germinal centre
    The pale centre of a lymphoid follicle, where activated B cells divide fast and switch and mutate their immunoglobulin genes. Burkitt lymphoma arises from a germinal centre B cell.
    Globi
    Clumps of many leprosy bacilli held together in lipid inside a macrophage. An acid-fast stain shows them in multibacillary leprosy.
    Glomerulosclerosis
    Scarring of glomeruli, in which matrix and collagen replace the capillary tuft. Segmental sclerosis involves part of a glomerulus; global sclerosis involves all of it.
    Gluconeogenesis
    The making of new glucose, mainly in the liver, from sources such as lactate and amino acids. Insulin suppresses it, and metformin reduces it.
    GLUT4
    The glucose transporter of muscle and fat cells. Insulin moves it from inside the cell to the cell membrane, so these cells take up glucose.
    Glycation
    Binding of glucose to a protein without an enzyme. The higher the blood glucose, the faster it happens.
    Granuloma
    A compact collection of epithelioid macrophages, often with multinucleate giant cells, ringed by lymphocytes.
    Grenz zone
    A narrow clear band of dermis between the epidermis and the infiltrate beneath it. It's seen in lepromatous leprosy.
    Grey baby syndrome
    Chloramphenicol toxicity in a newborn, who can't yet conjugate the drug with glucuronic acid. The drug builds up and causes vomiting, flaccidity, hypothermia, grey skin, shock and circulatory collapse.
    Growth fraction
    The share of a tumour's cells that are actively passing through the cell cycle. In Burkitt lymphoma it's nearly all of them.
    H antigen
    The protein that makes up a bacterial flagellum. Only motile bacteria carry it, and Salmonella Typhi is motile.
    HACEK group
    Haemophilus, Actinobacillus, Cardiobacterium, Eikenella and Kingella: commensals of the mouth that are less common causes of infective endocarditis.
    Haemoglobinopathy
    An inherited disorder caused by a mutation that changes the structure of a globin chain, such as sickle haemoglobin (Hb S) or Hb C.
    Haemosiderin
    A golden-brown pigment of stored iron, seen where red cells have broken down or iron is in excess.
    Haemozoin (malarial pigment)
    A brown-black pigment the parasite leaves from the haemoglobin it digests. Ruptured red cells release it, and macrophages in the spleen, liver and marrow take it up.
    Hand-foot syndrome
    Painful redness and swelling of the palms and soles, a skin toxicity of capecitabine and 5-fluorouracil.
    Haptoglobin
    A plasma protein that binds free haemoglobin. The pair is removed from the circulation, so the haptoglobin level falls in haemolysis.
    Haemoglobin electrophoresis
    A test that moves haemoglobins along a strip in an electric field, so each kind shows as a band at its own position.
    Heart failure cell
    A macrophage in the alveoli laden with haemosiderin from red cells that leaked out of congested lung capillaries.
    Heinz body
    Oxidised, denatured haemoglobin that has precipitated inside a red cell. A supravital stain, as used for reticulocytes, shows it.
    Hepcidin
    A hormone that keeps iron inside macrophages and blocks its absorption from the gut. Inflammation raises it, which starves the marrow of iron.
    Heterophile antibody
    An antibody made in infectious mononucleosis that agglutinates sheep red cells. The Monospot test detects it.
    HIV-associated nephropathy (HIVAN)
    Kidney disease caused by HIV: collapsing focal segmental glomerulosclerosis, with cystic dilated tubules and interstitial inflammation.
    Holoendemic malaria
    Malaria transmission that runs almost all year, so children are bitten by infected mosquitoes again and again from infancy.
    Howell-Jolly body
    A small remnant of DNA inside a red cell. Howell-Jolly bodies show on the blood film when the spleen has stopped working.
    High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC)
    A test that passes haemoglobin through a column. Each kind leaves at its own time, so the machine names it and measures its share of the total.
    HRP2 (histidine-rich protein 2)
    A protein made by P. falciparum and no other species, and the antigen most malaria rapid diagnostic tests detect.
    Hyaline arteriolosclerosis
    Thickening of an arteriole's wall by glassy, pink, structureless material leaked from the plasma, which narrows the lumen. Hypertension, age and diabetes mellitus cause it.
    Hyperfiltration
    Higher blood flow and capillary pressure in the surviving glomeruli after nephrons are lost. It holds up the GFR for a time but injures the glomeruli.
    Hyperplasia
    An increase in the number of cells in a tissue, in cells that can divide.
    Hypersplenism
    An enlarged spleen with a fall in at least one blood cell line while the bone marrow works normally.
    Hypertrophy
    An increase in the size of cells, and so of the organ, without more cells.
    Hypnozoite
    A dormant liver form of P. vivax and P. ovale. Hypnozoites can wake weeks or years later and cause a relapse.
    Hypogammaglobulinaemia
    A low level of immunoglobulins (antibodies) in the plasma. It makes infections more likely, bacterial ones first.
    Hypoglycaemia unawareness
    Loss of the autonomic warning signs of a low glucose after frequent episodes of hypoglycaemia, so the patient doesn't act until confusion or coma sets in.
    Hypoxia
    Too little oxygen reaching the cells.
    Hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF)
    A transcription factor that switches on erythropoietin production when oxygen is low. With oxygen present, HIF-1α is broken down; in hypoxia it survives and pairs with HIF-β.
    Idiosyncratic drug reaction
    A rare harmful drug effect that happens only in some people and is unrelated to the dose.
    Immunohistochemistry
    Staining a tissue section with labelled antibodies, so cells that carry a given protein turn brown. The tissue's architecture is kept.
    Immunophenotype
    The set of proteins a cell carries, shown with labelled antibodies. In a lymphoma it tells you which kind of lymphocyte the tumour copies, and at which stage.
    Impaired fasting glucose (IFG)
    A fasting venous plasma glucose from 6.1 up to 6.9 mmol/L, with a 2-hour OGTT glucose below 7.8. A stage between normal and diabetes mellitus.
    Impaired glucose tolerance (IGT)
    A 2-hour OGTT venous plasma glucose from 7.8 up to 11.1 mmol/L, with a fasting glucose below 7.0. Some people with it go on to develop diabetes mellitus.
    Incretin
    A gut hormone released after a meal that boosts insulin release from the β cells. GLP-1 and GIP are the two main incretins, and DPP-4 breaks them down.
    Infarct
    An area of ischaemic necrosis caused by loss of the blood supply, in sickle cell disease usually from vessels blocked by sickled cells.
    International normalised ratio (INR)
    The prothrombin time corrected for the thromboplastin reagent used, so a result means the same in any laboratory. A normal INR is about 1.
    Insulin resistance
    Failure of target tissues, mainly the liver, skeletal muscle and fat, to respond normally to insulin.
    Insulitis
    Infiltration of the pancreatic islets by lymphocytes, mainly T cells, while they destroy the β cells in type 1 diabetes.
    Intercalation
    Binding of a flat drug molecule between neighbouring base pairs of DNA, which blocks DNA and RNA synthesis. Anthracyclines such as doxorubicin intercalate.
    Intermittent preventive therapy
    Treatment doses of an antimalarial given at set times to people at high risk, such as pregnant women, whether or not they're infected.
    Interstitial fibrosis
    Collagen laid down in the tissue between the renal tubules, usually with atrophy and loss of the tubules.
    Intrathecal
    Injected into the CSF, so a drug reaches tumour cells in the central nervous system that drugs in the blood barely reach.
    Iron chelation
    Treatment with a drug that binds iron tightly so it can leave the body in urine or faeces. It treats the iron overload of repeated blood transfusion.
    Irreversibly sickled cell
    A red cell whose membrane has been damaged by many cycles of sickling, so it stays sickled even when it's carrying oxygen.
    Ischaemia
    Too little blood reaching a tissue: less oxygen, and less of everything else the blood brings and takes away.
    Isoenzyme
    One of several forms of the same enzyme, made by different tissues. Separating the isoenzymes of ALP shows whether it comes from bone, liver, gut or placenta.
    Karyolysis
    A nucleus fading away as its DNA is broken down.
    Karyorrhexis
    A pyknotic nucleus breaking into fragments.
    Karyotype
    A picture of all a cell's chromosomes, laid out in pairs. It needs dividing cells, so the cells are grown in culture first.
    ATP-sensitive potassium channel
    A potassium channel in the β cell membrane that closes when ATP rises. Closing it depolarises the cell and leads to insulin release. Sulfonylureas close it directly.
    Diabetic ketoacidosis
    Metabolic acidosis from ketone bodies, which the liver makes from fatty acids when there's too little insulin to stop fat breaking down.
    Ki-67
    A nuclear protein present in cells that are in the cell cycle. A Ki-67 stain gives the share of tumour cells dividing, called the proliferation index.
    Kussmaul breathing
    Deep, sighing breathing in metabolic acidosis. It blows off carbon dioxide, which lessens the fall in pH.
    Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH)
    An enzyme that turns lactate into pyruvate and back. It's found in many tissues and leaks into the plasma from damaged cells, so a raised level shows cell damage but not where.
    Lactic acidosis
    A high anion gap metabolic acidosis from lactate building up in the blood. Its commonest cause is tissue short of oxygen, from poor perfusion, hypoxia or severe anaemia.
    Lagophthalmos
    Inability to close the eyelids fully, from damage to the facial nerve. The eye is left exposed and can be injured.
    Lancefield group
    A letter (A, B, D and others) given to a streptococcus by the C carbohydrate antigen in its cell wall.
    Langhans giant cell
    A multinucleate cell formed when macrophages fuse. Its nuclei sit in a horseshoe around the edge of the cell.
    Latency I
    The most restricted pattern of Epstein-Barr virus gene expression, with EBNA1 and the EBER RNAs and little else. Burkitt lymphoma cells show this pattern.
    Latent infection
    An infection in which the viral DNA stays inside the cell and makes few or no new virus particles. Epstein-Barr virus keeps its DNA in the nucleus as a circle.
    Leonine facies
    Thick, lumpy skin over the whole face, which looks like a lion's. A sign of lepromatous leprosy.
    Lepra cell (Virchow cell)
    A macrophage with pale, foamy cytoplasm, packed with leprosy bacilli. Sheets of lepra cells fill the dermis in lepromatous leprosy.
    Lepromin test
    A skin test that injects Mycobacterium leprae antigens and reads the delayed hypersensitivity response to them. It's strongly positive in tuberculoid leprosy and negative in lepromatous leprosy.
    Leucopenia
    A fall in the number of white cells in the blood, below the reference range.
    Leukaemic infiltration
    Leukaemic cells spreading into organs outside the marrow, such as the liver, spleen, lymph nodes, meninges, gums, skin or testes, and enlarging or damaging them.
    Leukaemoid reaction
    A reactive rise in white cells high enough to look like leukaemia, with immature granulocytes in the blood. Severe infection, severe haemolysis and cancer cause it.
    Light chain restriction
    Expression of only kappa or only lambda light chain by a population of B cells. Light chain restriction shows the cells are one clone: each B cell makes one light chain, but a normal, polyclonal population carries both, kappa more often than lambda.
    Lipofuscin
    A yellow-brown 'wear and tear' pigment of oxidised lipid, building up in long-lived cells with age.
    Lipohypertrophy
    A swelling of fatty tissue under the skin where insulin is injected into the same site again and again. Insulin is absorbed unevenly from it.
    Liquefactive necrosis
    Necrosis in which enzymes digest the dead tissue into a liquid.
    M cell
    A specialised epithelial cell over a Peyer's patch that samples what's in the gut lumen and passes it to the lymphoid tissue beneath. Salmonella Typhi uses this route to cross the lining of the ileum.
    M protein
    A major virulence factor of group A streptococcus: a wall protein that blocks complement activation and protects the organism from phagocytosis.
    Macrolide
    A class of antibiotics, such as erythromycin and clarithromycin, that bind the 50S subunit of the bacterial ribosome and stop protein synthesis.
    Malignant otitis externa
    Pseudomonas aeruginosa infection of the external ear canal that burrows into the mastoid bone, mostly in older people with diabetes mellitus.
    Marrow failure
    Loss of the marrow's normal output of red cells, neutrophils and platelets. In leukaemia the clone crowds out normal blood-forming cells, causing anaemia, infection and bleeding.
    Merozoite
    The form of Plasmodium released when an infected liver cell or red cell bursts. Each merozoite invades a red cell.
    Metabolic syndrome
    A cluster of central obesity, high triglyceride, low HDL cholesterol, raised blood pressure and raised fasting glucose, linked by insulin resistance.
    Metaplasia
    The replacement of one mature cell type by another, better able to stand a particular stress.
    Metastatic calcification
    Calcium deposited in normal tissue because the blood calcium is high.
    Methaemoglobinaemia
    Haemoglobin in the blood with its iron oxidised from the Fe2+ to the Fe3+ state. Drugs such as primaquine can cause it, and the patient looks cyanosed.
    Microalbuminuria
    A urine albumin of 30 to 300 mg a day, too little for overt proteinuria. In diabetes mellitus it's one of the earliest signs of nephropathy.
    Microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia
    Destruction of red cells as they pass through damaged small vessels, often across fibrin strands in DIC. The blood film shows many red cell fragments.
    Diabetic microangiopathy
    Disease of small blood vessels in diabetes mellitus: capillary basement membranes become thick, yet the capillaries leak plasma proteins.
    Middle molecules
    Small compounds the kidneys normally excrete. They build up in kidney failure and may cause some features of uraemia.
    Molecular mimicry
    An immune response to a microbe that also attacks the body's own tissue, because a microbial antigen looks like a self antigen.
    Monocytosis
    A rise in the number of monocytes in the blood. Chronic bacterial infections, such as tuberculosis, brucellosis and typhoid fever, are among its causes.
    Mucormycosis (zygomycosis)
    Infection with moulds such as Rhizopus and Mucor, whose hyphae have no septa, branch at right angles and invade blood vessel walls.
    Multidrug-resistant (MDR)
    Resistant to antibiotics from several different classes at once, such as methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus.
    Multidrug-resistant (MDR) typhoid
    Salmonella Typhi that resists chloramphenicol, ampicillin and co-trimoxazole together.
    Multidrug therapy (MDT)
    Treatment of leprosy with rifampicin, dapsone and clofazimine taken together, for 6 months in paucibacillary leprosy and 12 months in multibacillary leprosy.
    Mural thrombus
    A thrombus attached to the wall of a heart chamber or of the aorta.
    Mycolactone
    The lipid toxin of Mycobacterium ulcerans. It kills cells, holds back the local immune response and dulls pain, which is why a Buruli ulcer enlarges painlessly with little inflammation.
    Mycotic aneurysm
    An aneurysm caused by bacterial infection of an artery wall, where an infected embolus has lodged.
    Myeloid sarcoma
    A solid mass of myeloid blasts outside the bone marrow, also called granulocytic sarcoma or chloroma. The marrow is often involved at the same time.
    Myeloperoxidase (MPO)
    An enzyme in the granules of myeloid cells. A stain for it is positive in myeloid blasts and negative in lymphoblasts.
    Myelosuppression (marrow suppression)
    Suppression of the bone marrow, so fewer blood cells are made. Most cytotoxic drugs cause it, because marrow cells divide fast. Neutropenia is the usual result.
    Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase (the sodium pump)
    The membrane pump that moves sodium out of a cell and potassium in, using ATP. Digoxin inhibits it in heart muscle, and potassium competes with digoxin for it.
    Necrosis
    Death of cells in a living tissue, with their membranes breaking and their contents leaking out, which brings in inflammation.
    Necrotising granuloma
    A granuloma whose centre has died. In tuberculosis the dead centre is caseous.
    Nephron
    The working unit of the kidney: a glomerulus, which filters the plasma, and the tubule that drains it.
    Nephrosclerosis
    Diffuse scarring of the kidney from narrowed arteries and arterioles, which leaves a finely granular surface and a thin cortex.
    Neuritis
    Inflammation of a nerve. In leprosy, granulomas or bacilli in a nerve swell it, make it tender and can destroy its fibres.
    Neurogenic bladder
    A bladder that doesn't empty properly because the nerves that control it are damaged. In diabetes mellitus autonomic neuropathy causes it, and urine left standing lets bacteria multiply.
    Neuroglycopenia
    Brain dysfunction from too little glucose: faintness, dizziness and confusion, which can progress to coma.
    Neutropenia
    A fall in the number of neutrophils in the blood, below the reference range.
    Neutrophil leucocytosis (neutrophilia)
    A rise in the number of neutrophils in the blood. Acute bacterial infection, especially pyogenic infection, is its commonest cause.
    NKCC2 (Na⁺/K⁺/2Cl⁻ cotransporter)
    The transporter on the luminal side of the thick ascending limb of the loop of Henle that reabsorbs sodium, potassium and chloride together. Loop diuretics such as furosemide block it.
    Nodular glomerulosclerosis (Kimmelstiel-Wilson lesion)
    Ball-like nodules of laminated matrix at the edge of the glomerulus, almost pathognomonic of diabetes mellitus.
    Non-Hodgkin lymphoma
    Any malignant tumour of lymphocytes other than Hodgkin lymphoma. Most arise from B cells, and Burkitt lymphoma is one of them.
    Non-lactose fermenter
    A bacterium that can't break lactose down to acid, so its colonies stay pale on a medium whose indicator changes colour with acid. Salmonella and Shigella are non-lactose fermenters; Escherichia coli isn't.
    Nontuberculous mycobacteria
    Acid-fast mycobacteria other than Mycobacterium tuberculosis and Mycobacterium leprae. Most live in soil and water, and Mycobacterium ulcerans causes Buruli ulcer.
    O antigen
    The outermost sugar chain of the lipopolysaccharide in the wall of a Gram-negative bacterium. Its sugars differ between organisms, so it's used to group them.
    Oliguria
    A urine output below 400 mL a day in an adult. It usually means a low GFR.
    Oncogene
    A gene that drives cell growth and division when it's mutated or overexpressed. MYC is the oncogene switched on in Burkitt lymphoma.
    Opsonisation
    Coating of a microbe with antibody or complement so phagocytes can recognise and ingest it.
    Osmotic diuresis
    A large flow of urine caused by a solute, such as glucose, that stays in the renal tubule and holds water with it.
    Osteomyelitis
    Infection of bone and its marrow. In sickle cell disease bacteria seed bone already damaged by infarction.
    Osteonecrosis of the jaw
    Death of an area of bone in the jaw. A rare adverse effect of bisphosphonates and denosumab, commoner with the high intravenous doses used for bone metastases.
    Ototoxicity
    Damage to the inner ear by a drug: tinnitus and hearing loss, or vertigo and loss of balance. Aminoglycosides and loop diuretics cause it, more so when renal function is poor.
    Overflow proteinuria
    Small proteins in the urine because the blood carries more of them than the tubules can take back, as with Bence Jones protein, haemoglobin or myoglobin.
    P-selectin
    An adhesion molecule that helps sickle cells stick to the walls of blood vessels, one step in vaso-occlusion.
    Pancarditis
    Inflammation of all three layers of the heart: the pericardium, the myocardium and the endocardium, including the valves.
    Pancytopenia
    A fall in all three blood cell lines: red cells, white cells and platelets.
    Panniculitis
    Inflammation of the fat beneath the skin.
    Papillary necrosis (necrotising papillitis)
    Death of the renal papillae, the tips of the medullary pyramids, as a form of acute pyelonephritis. Diabetes mellitus, urinary obstruction and sickle cell anaemia are its main settings.
    Paradoxical reaction (Buruli ulcer)
    Worsening of a Buruli ulcer during effective treatment. As the bacilli die and mycolactone falls, the inflammatory response returns.
    Paraprotein
    One immunoglobulin made in excess by a single clone of B cells. Serum protein electrophoresis shows it as a narrow, dense band, usually in the gamma region.
    Parasitaemia
    Malaria parasites in the circulating blood, counted on a film as the share of red cells infected or the number per microlitre.
    Peau d'orange
    Thickened, finely pitted skin over a breast cancer, like orange peel. Tumour blocks the lymphatics of the dermis, so the skin swells with oedema.
    Penicillin-binding protein (PBP)
    A bacterial enzyme that cross-links the peptide chains of peptidoglycan, which gives the cell wall its strength. Beta-lactam antibiotics bind its active site and block it.
    Perforation
    A hole through the full thickness of the wall of a hollow organ, such as the ileum, so that its contents leak out.
    Peritonitis
    Inflammation of the peritoneum. When the ileum perforates, leaked bowel contents cause faecal peritonitis.
    Peyer's patch
    A collection of lymphoid tissue in the wall of the small intestine, prominent in the terminal ileum. M cells in the epithelium over it take up material from the lumen.
    Philadelphia chromosome
    The short chromosome 22 left by t(9;22). It carries the BCR-ABL1 fusion gene and is found in nearly all chronic myeloid leukaemia and in a minority of acute lymphoblastic leukaemia.
    Phocomelia
    A birth defect in which the long bones of the limbs are shortened or absent. Thalidomide causes it when taken in early pregnancy.
    Phosphate binder
    A drug taken with food that binds phosphate in the gut lumen, so the phosphate leaves in the stool instead of being absorbed. Calcium carbonate, calcium acetate, sevelamer and lanthanum carbonate are examples.
    Pigment gallstone
    A gallstone made of calcium bilirubinate. It forms when bile carries a high load of unconjugated bilirubin, as in chronic haemolysis.
    pLDH (Plasmodium lactate dehydrogenase)
    An enzyme of the malaria parasite, detected by some rapid diagnostic tests. pLDH tests turn negative within days of cure.
    Polyclonal
    Coming from many different B-cell clones at once, rather than from one. Malaria and early Epstein-Barr virus infection both drive polyclonal B-cell activation.
    Polyclonal response
    Immunoglobulins made by many B-cell clones at once, as in chronic infection. Serum protein electrophoresis shows it as a broad, diffuse rise in the gamma region.
    Polymerisation of Hb S
    Deoxygenated sickle haemoglobin molecules binding one another into long fibres. The fibres distort the red cell into a sickle shape.
    Polymicrobial infection
    Infection with more than one organism at the same time. Infection that spreads into tissue from an open wound is often polymicrobial.
    Polyol (sorbitol) pathway
    Conversion of glucose to sorbitol by aldose reductase. It uses up NADPH, so glutathione can't be regenerated and the cell is left open to oxidative stress.
    PPAR-γ
    Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma, a nuclear receptor in muscle, fat and liver that controls genes for glucose and fat handling. Pioglitazone activates it.
    Prodrug
    A drug given in an inactive form that the body converts into the active drug. Cyclophosphamide is one: liver cytochrome P450 enzymes activate it.
    Proliferation centre
    A pale, ill-defined focus of larger dividing cells among the sheets of small lymphocytes in a lymph node. Proliferation centres are pathognomonic of chronic lymphocytic leukaemia and small lymphocytic lymphoma.
    Prothrombin time (PT)
    The time citrated plasma takes to clot after thromboplastin and calcium are added. It tests the extrinsic and common clotting pathways.
    Pseudohyperkalaemia
    A potassium result raised in the tube, not in the patient: potassium leaks out of cells after the blood is taken, as in a haemolysed sample or one left unseparated.
    Pseudohypoglycaemia
    A falsely low glucose result, because blood cells kept using glucose in a tube with no fluoride before the plasma was separated.
    Parathyroid hormone-related protein (PTHrP)
    A protein, normally made in small amounts by many tissues, that some tumours make in excess. Its active end resembles PTH, so it acts on the PTH receptor. It isn't switched off by a high calcium, and the PTH assay doesn't detect it.
    Pure red cell aplasia
    Failure of the marrow to make red cells while white cells and platelets are made normally. On an ESA, antibodies that neutralise erythropoietin cause it.
    Purine
    A nitrogen-containing base of DNA and RNA: adenine or guanine. In humans, purines that aren't reused are broken down to urate.
    Pyknosis
    A nucleus that has shrunk and stains dark, as its chromatin condenses.
    Pyuria
    Neutrophils in the urine, which can make it look turbid. It's a sign of urinary infection such as acute pyelonephritis.
    Radical cure
    Treatment that clears malaria parasites from both the blood and the liver, so P. vivax or P. ovale can't relapse. It needs a blood-stage drug plus primaquine or tafenoquine.
    RANKL (RANK ligand)
    A signal made by osteoblasts and other cells that binds RANK on osteoclasts and their precursors, so osteoclasts form and resorb bone. Denosumab is an antibody against it.
    Recrudescence
    The return of malaria from blood-stage parasites that treatment didn't clear. A relapse is different: it comes from dormant liver forms.
    Relative bradycardia
    A pulse slower than the height of the fever would lead you to expect. It's a recognised sign of typhoid fever.
    Renal osteodystrophy
    The bone disease of chronic kidney disease, a mixture of osteitis fibrosa from excess PTH and osteomalacia from too little active vitamin D.
    Renal threshold for glucose
    The plasma glucose, about 10 mmol/L, above which the renal tubules can't reabsorb all the filtered glucose, so glucose appears in the urine.
    Ischaemia–reperfusion injury
    Further injury to ischaemic tissue when its blood supply comes back.
    Reticulocyte
    A young red cell, just out of the marrow, that still holds RNA. The reticulocyte count shows how well the marrow is making red cells.
    Reye syndrome
    An illness of children and adolescents linked to taking aspirin during a viral infection, with or without fever. Aspirin isn't given to children or adolescents with a viral illness.
    Ribonucleotide reductase
    The enzyme that turns ribonucleotides into the deoxyribonucleotides a cell needs to copy its DNA. Blocking it stops dividing cells in the S phase of the cell cycle.
    Richter transformation
    Change of chronic lymphocytic leukaemia into an aggressive high-grade B-cell lymphoma. A lymph node that suddenly grows fast is the usual sign.
    Ridley-Jopling classification
    The classification of leprosy into five groups along one immune spectrum: tuberculoid (TT), borderline tuberculoid (BT), mid-borderline (BB), borderline lepromatous (BL) and lepromatous (LL).
    Ring abscess
    An abscess in the tissue around a valve ring, where an infective vegetation has eroded into the myocardium beneath it.
    Ring haemorrhage
    A small haemorrhage in the brain's white matter arranged as a ring around a small vessel, seen at autopsy in cerebral malaria.
    Rose spots
    Small pink maculopapular spots on the chest and abdomen in typhoid fever. They're seen on light skin and are hard to see on dark skin.
    Rouleaux
    Red cells stacked like a pile of coins. Fibrinogen and immunoglobulins cause the stacking, and the stacks settle fast, raising the ESR.
    Saddle nose
    Collapse of the bridge of the nose after its cartilage is destroyed. In leprosy it's a sign of the lepromatous form.
    Sanctuary site
    A tissue that drugs in the blood reach poorly, such as the brain and the testes, so tumour cells there can survive treatment.
    Schizont
    A Plasmodium form whose nucleus has divided many times. It breaks up into merozoites when the liver cell or red cell bursts.
    Schüffner's dots
    Fine dots in a red cell infected with P. vivax or P. ovale, seen on a well-stained Giemsa film as the parasite matures.
    Schwann cell
    The cell that wraps the axons of peripheral nerves in myelin. Mycobacterium leprae enters it by binding laminin-α2 in its basal lamina.
    Secondary hyperparathyroidism
    Raised PTH secreted by healthy parathyroid glands in response to a low free ionised calcium, as in chronic kidney disease or vitamin D deficiency.
    Senescence
    A permanent stop to cell division that cells reach with age.
    Sentinel lymph node
    The first regional lymph node that receives lymph from a tumour. If it's free of tumour, the lymph nodes beyond it usually are too.
    Sequestration
    Red cells carrying maturing P. falciparum sticking to the endothelium of small vessels, through the parasite protein PfEMP1, so they leave the circulating blood and block the capillaries.
    Selective oestrogen receptor modulator (SERM)
    A drug that binds the oestrogen receptor and blocks it in some tissues but acts like oestrogen in others. Tamoxifen blocks it in the breast and acts like oestrogen in bone and the uterus.
    SGLT2 (sodium-glucose co-transporter 2)
    The transporter in the proximal tubule that reabsorbs most of the glucose filtered by the kidney. SGLT2 inhibitors block it, so glucose is lost in the urine.
    Shift to the left
    More band forms in the blood, and sometimes earlier cells such as metamyelocytes and myelocytes. It's seen in a reactive neutrophil leucocytosis.
    Siderofibrotic nodule
    A small nodule of fibrous tissue holding iron pigment and calcium, found in the scarred spleen of sickle cell disease beside vessels full of sickled cells.
    Hepatic sinusoidal lymphocytosis
    Lymphocytes filling the sinusoids of the liver on biopsy. It supports a diagnosis of tropical splenomegaly syndrome but isn't specific to it.
    Slit-skin smear
    A test for leprosy. The skin is pinched, a small slit is cut, the cut edge is scraped, and the scraping is smeared on a slide and stained by Ziehl-Neelsen to look for acid-fast bacilli.
    Smear cell (smudge cell)
    A lymphocyte broken while the blood film is spread, seen as a smudge of nuclear material. Many smear cells point to chronic lymphocytic leukaemia, whose cells are fragile.
    Solubility product
    The highest product of two ion concentrations that a solution can hold. Above it, the salt comes out of solution, as calcium phosphate does when plasma phosphate is high.
    Somatic hypermutation
    Random mutation of the variable-region genes of immunoglobulin in dividing germinal centre B cells. Cells whose new antibody binds antigen better are selected to survive.
    Pitting
    The spleen's removal of inclusions, such as Howell-Jolly bodies and Heinz bodies, from red cells it then lets go back into the blood.
    Splenic sequestration
    Sudden pooling of sickled red cells in the spleen, which enlarges while the haemoglobin falls. Seen mostly in infants with sickle cell disease.
    Splenomegaly
    Enlargement of the spleen. A massive spleen can reach the right iliac fossa.
    Sporozoite
    The form of Plasmodium that an infected female Anopheles mosquito injects when it bites. Sporozoites travel in the blood to the liver.
    Starry-sky pattern
    Pale tingible body macrophages scattered through sheets of dark tumour cells, seen at low power. Typical of Burkitt lymphoma, but not specific to it.
    Steatosis (fatty change)
    Triglyceride building up inside cells, seen most in the liver.
    Storage pool defect
    A shortage of the granules inside platelets, so platelets release too little of their granule contents when activated. Uraemia causes an acquired form.
    Streptolysin O
    An oxygen-labile toxin of group A streptococcus that lyses red and white cells. It's antigenic, so infection is followed by anti-streptolysin O antibodies.
    Sulfone
    A drug closely related to the sulfonamides that blocks folate synthesis in bacteria. Dapsone is the sulfone used in leprosy.
    Sydenham chorea
    A neurological disorder of acute rheumatic fever with involuntary, purposeless, rapid movements.
    Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase (TdT)
    A DNA polymerase made by precursor B and T cells, before they mature. A positive TdT stain marks a lymphoblast.
    Terminal duct lobular unit
    A breast lobule and the small duct that drains it. Almost all breast carcinomas arise from its cells.
    Tertiary hyperparathyroidism
    Parathyroid glands, enlarged by years of secondary hyperparathyroidism, that keep secreting PTH partly on their own, so the calcium rises and PTH isn't suppressed.
    Th1 cell
    A CD4 helper T cell that makes interferon-γ, which activates macrophages to kill the microbes they've eaten.
    Thymidylate synthase
    The enzyme that makes thymidylate, which cells need to build DNA. 5-fluorouracil and capecitabine inhibit it, so dividing cells can't copy their DNA.
    Thyroidisation
    Dilated renal tubules filled with glassy, PAS-positive colloid casts that look like thyroid follicles. A feature of chronic pyelonephritis.
    Tingible body macrophage
    A benign macrophage that has eaten the nuclear debris of apoptotic cells. In Burkitt lymphoma these macrophages form the pale stars of the starry sky.
    Tissue schizonticide
    An antimalarial drug that kills the parasites developing or lying dormant in the liver, such as primaquine.
    Topoisomerase II
    An enzyme that cuts both strands of DNA and joins them again, so DNA can unwind as it's copied. Anthracyclines such as daunorubicin inhibit it, and the DNA is left broken.
    Toxic granulation
    Coarse dark granules in the cytoplasm of neutrophils, seen with Döhle bodies in a reactive neutrophilia from infection or inflammation.
    Transferrin saturation
    Serum iron as a percentage of the total iron-binding capacity. It shows how much of the iron carrier in plasma holds iron on its way to the marrow.
    Chromosomal translocation
    A break in two chromosomes with an exchange of the broken pieces. In Burkitt lymphoma, t(8;14) moves MYC on chromosome 8 next to the immunoglobulin heavy-chain gene on chromosome 14.
    Trephine biopsy
    A solid core of bone and marrow, usually from the posterior iliac crest, fixed, decalcified and cut as a histology section. It shows the marrow's structure and how full of cells it is.
    Trophozoite
    The growing stage of Plasmodium inside a red cell. On a stained film the young trophozoite looks like a ring.
    Tubular proteinuria
    Small proteins in the urine, such as retinol-binding protein, because damaged proximal tubules fail to take back what the glomeruli normally filter.
    Tumour lysis syndrome
    The chemical upset when many tumour cells die at once, usually as treatment starts: urate, potassium and phosphate rise, calcium falls, and the kidneys can fail.
    Tumour marker
    A substance in the blood that a tumour makes or releases. In some tumours its level relates to the tumour's mass or spread, and a fall or a new rise can follow treatment and recurrence.
    Tumour suppressor gene
    A gene whose protein holds back cell growth or repairs DNA. A cancer arises only when both copies are lost or faulty.
    Type II hypersensitivity
    Tissue injury caused by IgG or IgM antibodies that bind antigens fixed on cells or in the tissue matrix.
    Type III hypersensitivity
    Tissue injury caused by antigen-antibody (immune) complexes that deposit in vessel walls or glomeruli and activate complement.
    Type IV (delayed) hypersensitivity
    Tissue injury caused by T cells. CD4 T cells release cytokines, mainly interferon-γ, which activate macrophages, and the reaction takes a day or more to develop.
    Typhoid conjugate vaccine
    A typhoid vaccine made of purified Vi antigen linked to a carrier protein, given as one injection from 6 months of age. It protects for longer than the older typhoid vaccines.
    Typhoid nodule
    A small focus of necrosis with an aggregate of macrophages, scattered at random through the liver, bone marrow and lymph nodes in typhoid fever.
    Tyrosine kinase inhibitor
    A drug that blocks a tyrosine kinase, an enzyme that passes on a growth signal by putting a phosphate from ATP onto its target proteins. Imatinib blocks BCR-ABL1 in chronic myeloid leukaemia.
    Unconjugated bilirubin
    Bilirubin that the liver hasn't yet joined to glucuronate. It isn't water-soluble, so it travels in the plasma bound to albumin and doesn't pass into the urine.
    Undermined ulcer
    An ulcer whose edge overhangs dead tissue beneath it, so the damage under the skin is wider than the hole. Typical of Buruli ulcer.
    Uraemia
    Azotaemia severe enough to cause symptoms and signs, such as vomiting, bleeding or pericarditis.
    Urobilinogen
    A colourless product that gut bacteria make from bilirubin. Some is absorbed and sent back into bile by the liver, and a little passes in the urine.
    Vaso-occlusion
    Blockage of small blood vessels by sickled red cells, which causes ischaemia, pain and infarction in the tissue they supply.
    Vegetation
    A thrombus on a heart valve. It may be sterile, or hold organisms, as in infective endocarditis.
    Verrucae (rheumatic vegetations)
    Small, warty vegetations in a row along the lines of closure of a valve in acute rheumatic fever, made of fibrinoid necrosis and fibrin.
    Vi antigen
    The polysaccharide capsule of Salmonella Typhi. It covers the O antigen and shields it from antibody, and the typhoid conjugate vaccine is made from it.
    Villous lymphocyte
    A lymphocyte with fine hair-like projections from its cytoplasm. Villous lymphocytes in the blood suggest splenic marginal zone lymphoma.
    Virchow's triad
    The three causes of thrombosis: endothelial injury, stasis or turbulent blood flow, and hypercoagulability of the blood.
    Viridans streptococci
    Alpha-haemolytic streptococci that are normal flora of the mouth. They're the usual cause of subacute infective endocarditis on a damaged valve.
    Vitamin K epoxide reductase (VKOR)
    The liver enzyme that turns used vitamin K epoxide back into active vitamin K, so clotting factors can go on being carboxylated. Warfarin blocks it. Its gene is VKORC1.
    Whipple's triad
    A low plasma glucose, neuroglycopenic symptoms, and relief of the symptoms when the glucose is raised. Together they show that symptoms come from hypoglycaemia.
    Xanthine oxidase
    The liver enzyme that oxidises hypoxanthine to xanthine, and xanthine to urate. Allopurinol inhibits it.
